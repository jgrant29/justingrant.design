The usability tests said it was fine. The classroom said otherwise.

McGraw Hill · ALEKS · UX Researcher2023

ALEKS is McGraw Hill's AI-driven learning platform. It works out what each student knows and assigns what to learn next, and teachers run class from its dashboard. Usability tests kept saying the dashboard was fine. I argued for going where the work happens, and what I saw in classrooms changed the dashboard and the roadmap.

Results

The harvest

The question

If the tool tests fine, why aren't teachers using it in class?

A usability test answers a narrow question: can a person finish this task on this screen? Teachers could. That told us the dashboard was usable. It did not tell us whether it was useful during a class period, with a full room and no time to study a screen.

So the question changed from "can teachers use the dashboard?" to "what do teachers do instead of using it?"

Method · Matched to the question

Real classrooms, real class periods

That question can't be answered in a lab, so I made the case for contextual inquiry and took the research into schools. Across a regional study with more than 100 teachers, I watched class periods and talked with teachers about what they did and why.

Field work shows what is happening. It can't show how common it is. To check whether the classroom findings held more widely, I analyzed more than 10,000 survey responses alongside them, and kept usability testing running in two-week sprints so the product teams had evidence at the pace they were building.

Findings

Teachers circled the room because the screen couldn't tell them who was stuck

  • The signal arrived too late. The dashboard reported on students after the fact. Teachers needed to know who was struggling while it was happening, so they walked the room to find out.
  • Teachers wanted explanations, not guesses. They wanted to see why a student got something wrong, not only that they had.
  • Students stalled at the start. They needed clearer direction on what to do first.
  • Teachers needed the last word. When the system assigned work automatically, teachers wanted to be able to override it.
What changed

A dashboard that works during class, and a roadmap with override on it

The dashboard was redesigned around real-time signals, including alerts that surface students who need support. Benchmarked with UMUX-Lite, its usability score rose from 73 to 82, a 12 percent lift.

I showed executives that teachers needed manual override of automated assignments, and it went onto the product roadmap.

Limits

What this evidence does and doesn't show

  • The score is a before-and-after benchmark. It shows the redesigned dashboard tested better. It does not show how much each change contributed.
  • The field study was regional. I reported it that way and used the survey data, not the classroom visits, for any claim about scale.

McGraw Hill's research materials are internal, so this page describes the work without screens, recordings, or participant data.
